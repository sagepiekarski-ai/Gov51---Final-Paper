1 Introduction

The National Hockey League is growing faster than ever before. With an average 15% year over year growth in each individual teams’ valuation, the average of team valuations has more than doubled from $1billion in 2022 to $2.2 billion in 2024. Certainly there is a spectrum of team valuations within these means, but both ends are experiencing large increases in their valuations. For instance, the Columbus Blue Jackets is the lowest valuated team in all of the NHL, yet there valuation has jumped 30% since 2024, reaching $1.3 billion. On the flip side, the Toronto Maple Leafs continue to be the highest valuated team in the league, growing 16% since 2024, to a value of $4.4 billion.

All things considered, as the NHL continues to skyrocket into unchartered territory, the stakes and performances of each one of the 32 franchises becomes increasingly important. While there is a bounty of data detailling NHL performance statistics, like MoneyPuck, modern day ice hockey statistics fail to provide complete predictive models of what players are most likely to have a standout season in the future.

To that end, this paper seeks to use past player performance statistics collected in MoneyPuck, to predict which NHL players have the highest probability of having a breakout season. Four questions guide the paper’s progression: how well do observable performance statistics predict breakout rate, does shrinkage using LASSO improve prediction compared to a traditional LPM or Logit model, does the model predict fairly across positions, and what players have had the largest predicted probability of having a breakout season in their next year of play.

We find that across three tested models an OLS Baseline, Logit, and LASSO, the Logit has the highest performance with a test RMSE of 0.3091 and a test accuracy of 54.8%. Using cross-validation to select a lambda for LASSO over Logit, did not prove to yield substantially different results. Within these models, we determine that shots per game is the strongest predictor of a breakout season next season, while games played, expected goals, and being a defender are negatively associated with future breakout. The Logit model does predict fairly evenly across positions with slight overprediction for centers and defenders and slight underprediction for right wings and left wings. Ultimately, our results reveal that while predicting breakout players is possible it is a very challenging feat to do accurately with our best model holding a 54.8% test accuracy.

2.2 Data Cleansing and Breakout Idenitifcation

The key variables used in our analysis from MoneyPuck are xgoals_per_game, games_played, and position. Based on MoneyPuck variables, we create goal_change, goals_per_game, xgoals_per_game, shots_per_game, and breakout. Below you will find an explanation of each of these variables.

xgoals_per_game - Expected goals per game for each player. MoneyPuck’s Expected Goals Model determines this variable, and determines it to be “the chance of an unblocked shot attempt being a goal. For example, a rebound shot in the slot may have a 50% of going in and be worth 0.5 expected goals, while a shot from the blueline while short handed may be worth 0.01 expected goals.”

games_played - Number of games played in a given season.

position - Identification of a player’s position: center, right wing, left wing, defenseman.

goal_change - A player’s next season goals minus current season goals.

goals_per_game - Average goals scored per game by a player in a given season.

xgoals_per_game - Average expected goals scored per game by a player in a given season.

shots_per_game - Number of shots on goal per game by a player in a given season.

breakout - binary variable, breakout players (1) are those players that rank in the top 10% of goal change and (0) if otherwise. In creating this variable, we decide to impose the 10 percent threshold as it is a reasonable requirement to identify those that are playing exceptionally well and have experienced high growth in performance since last season.

#clean the data
player_year <- skaters_2008_to_2024 %>% 
  filter(situation == "all") %>% 
  arrange(playerId, season) %>% 
  group_by(playerId) %>% 
  mutate(next_goals = lead(I_F_goals),
         goal_change = next_goals - I_F_goals) %>% 
  ungroup() %>% 
  filter(!is.na(goal_change), games_played>0) %>% 
  mutate(goals_per_game = I_F_goals / games_played,
        shots_per_game = I_F_shotsOnGoal / games_played,
        xgoals_per_game = I_F_xGoals / games_played)

#define a breakout player
breakout_threshold <- quantile(player_year$goal_change, 0.90, na.rm = TRUE)
player_year <- player_year %>% 
  mutate(breakout = ifelse(goal_change >= breakout_threshold, 1, 0))

We then bucket goals into four groups: 0-5, 6-15, 16-30, and 30+. Organizing data in such a manner allows us to see the direct association between goals scored this season and breakout rate percentage. This is a really important finding that alters later interpretations of our results. As such, the “Breakout Rate by Current Season Goal Table” illuminates the fact that as goals scored increases beyond 6-15, predicted probability of a breakout season decreases. In this way, those that scored an average of 0 to 5 goals, had an average breakout rate percentage of 9.4, those that scored 6-15 had a 15.1 percentage, those that scored 16-30 had a 13.7 percentage rate, and those that scored 30+ goals had the lowest average breakout rate at 7.8 percent.

This observed trend is further demonstrated in the above correlations table. As such, goals scored is negatively correlated with goal change, or the difference between a player’s next season goals and their current season goals. My personal hypothesis backing these findings is that I would suppose players that have reached an exceedingly high level of play, and have scored lots of goals, have a harder time substantially increasing their next season’s play. It might be easy to increase playing performance when one starts at a low point, but those players that are recognized as being strong, are marked more heavily, and have more of the other team’s attention on them, so this negative correlation does seem intuitive. On the other hand, next season goals correlates positively with the goal change variable. This makes sense given the consturuction of goal_change with next_goals being a component within it, so as next_goals increases so too should goal_change.

3.1 Train/Test Split

In an effort to mitigate the risk of overfitting, we create a training and test split of the data. As such, we group 70% of the data into a training set and the other 30% of the data into a test set. This allows us to train our model on known data and still be able to analyse its performance on new data. In this way, our training set consists of 8,670 observations and the test set has 3,716 observations. The training set is used for model fit, while the test set is used specifically to evaluate the model’s performance.

Given that our outcome variable, breakout, is a binary variable, this is identified as a Linear Probability Model. The according outputs reveal that shots on goal is positively associated with being a breakout player. On the other hand, games played and expected goals are negatively associated with being a breakout player. In terms of position, being a defenseman is associated with a 12.5 percentage points decrease in the probability of becoming breakout player.

It is also intriguing to note from our actual and predicted table that 1928 + 392 = 2,320 predicted breakout players out of a test set of 3,716. 2,320 / 3,716 = 0.6243, so our model predicts that about 62.43% of skaters will be breakout players. This is a very large percentage of players especially considering the actual breakout rate from our first summary statistics table was 11.7%. The major downside of this type of model, is that with a binary Y and continuous regressors, our regression can produce values that don’t lie between 0 and 1.

6 Limitations

One of the most obvious limitations of this paper is omitted variable bias. One such omitted variable could be player compatibility. Hockey players take extremely short shifts, and practice together for extended amounts of time, so their productivity may differ depending on who they are on a line with. This study fails to acknowledge the important aspect that some players simply work better together than others, and coaching decision and quality to execute good lines proves to be another omitted variable on top of that.

Applied to this study, player compatibility would impact the breakout dependent variable, as players will play better if they are working with those they have a connection with and this means they could have an increased goal_change difference. Considering the OVB formula α1 = β1 + β2γ1, the sign of β2 is likely positive. In addition, player compatibility is correlated with a regressor of interest, like shots per game, given that if you are playing with someone you gel well with, there can be an aspect of telephathy where one player knows where the other will be before they are even there. This relationship allows for heightened passing and shooting opportunites, and γ1 would thus have a positive sign. All of this considered, the net effect of β2γ1 is positive. We have overstated the true effect, estimating an effect (α1) larger in magnitude than that of the true effect (β1).

It is also important to note that we do not have a complete understanding of the model MoneyPuck uses to generate an expected goals value. Tanner specifices that the model determines this variable via computing the chance that an unblocked shot attempt becomes a goal. For instance, 0.5 expected goals could come from a rebound shot in the slot that has a 50% chance of becoming a goal, and 0.01 expected goals could result from a shot from the blueline while short handed. Ultimately, while compelling, the information stemming from this variable must be interpreted with a grain of salt given the lack of total understanding of its creation without the specific coding creation made public.

The results of this paper do not suggest causation. The purpose is to provide a predictive model of future breakout players. For future alterations of this project I would look to try to incorporate hard to quantiy variables by using fixed effects. Reflecting on the variables included, I do believe it would be beneficial to add in a variable for ice time given the fact that shifts in hockey are so short and inconsistent. It could also be interesting to explore what these outcomes may have looked like if the 10% threshold on breakout had been larger. I would assume under this scenario we may observe more accurate prediction on test sets.

7 Conclusion

In the end, this paper seeked to explore whether one could predict which players in the NHL will have a breakout season in the following year using statistical performance data from a given season. We conclude that this process is possible, although extremely challenging to do accurately. Given the substantial amount of variables difficult to quantify and potential confounding factors, its important to acknowledge the relevant limitations of the created models, when interpreting our findings.

On the larger scale, current NHL statistics provide many predictive models of who will win games and previous sports statistics on player performance. However, there is a lack of understanding of whether we can predict which players will excel in next season’s performance in relation to their performance this past season. Predictive player modelling, as is done in this paper, will provide extremely telling insight into which player’s are currently undervalued in the league. As the NHL continues to grow in popularity, revenue, and valuation, the stakes continue to rise, making the impact of predictive player statistics increasingly important.
